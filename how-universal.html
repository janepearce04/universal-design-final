How to implement accessibility?

There are four primary kinds of disability that are considered when building a webpage: auditory, visual, mobility/dexterity, and cognitive. Given these are the primary categories of impairment and a fair 1/7 of the population deals with the struggles of daily life experiencing one or multiple kinds of limitations, the last thing we want is to make navigating our content difficult, especially when making it accessible is often not trivial and fairly easy. Having a basis for accessibility and a framework to understanding what makes something accessible is crucial for building an accessible website from the ground up. Including all kinds of content mediums is one step towards making a more easily understandable and usable website.

Another consideration is what kind of technology this content hinges on to make work. If your product works well and is tested only as a laptop, then the translation to a mobile webpage may be difficult, as well as something like an ipad or another kind of screened device that doesn’t have a keyboard to input anything into. For a user that relies on a screen-reader to navigate through a website, the visual appeal is not a concern so much but the semantic layout and aria-labels are crucial pieces of information. For anything that might not be apparent as read by the screen reader, additional instructions can be provided without changing the layout or look of the page at all.

You can see the difference between a cluttered webpage and a simple page, and while one sacrifices a small amount of control, spacing things out makes a webpage much morse usable.

This however does not dismiss the look and design of a webpage as important; just the converse. For users with cognitive disabilities or neurodivergent tendencies, having not only a simple and non-eggregious visual design but a predictable layout can make the difference between an hour long, frustrating, excruciating website trial and error process, versus a 5-minute search for a webpage. Navigation, summaries, and simple language are your best friends in this case, to give not only a piece of content that is engaging initially, but also enables those who have limitations to find what they need more quickly and streamlines the process for users who are unfamiliar with your product. This is what we like to call universal design– as it can be usable for essentially anyone who has technology and a web connection.
