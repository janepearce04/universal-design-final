Why is accessibility important?

In our age of technology, accessibility is important not only for the sake of those who need to use technology but also for all of those around them. When someone in a project team or crew can’t do the work because they’re held back by their body or mind in some way, you wouldn’t expect it of them to just keep working when they can’t access a necessary part of the process. It’s the same with website functionality. If it can only be used and understood judged on one medium, there’s a large scale demographic who might access your website and are unable to proceed because you’ve missed a simple visual cue or an aria label that describes crucial instructions.

Good aria label and bad aria label example.

This is how the internet should function: information accessible and understandable to all. The four core tenants of w3c’s accessibility guidelines are perception, operation, understandability, and robustness, which remarkably has a fair amount of overlap with Don Norman’s design principles which include: Affordance, constraints, feedback, and visibility. In some ways, these principles point towards the same issue: effective communication, and in most ways, communication that is understandable by everybody is thorough, concise, and is the most effective way to convey ideas.

Accessibility is simply good business; it expands your reachable demographics, improves the user experience for everybody, and enables you to implement things further down the line. Additionally, while the translation from one medium to another on its face value may be sufficient sometimes, things are not always stressed the same nor understood the same. This matters because the interpretation is not the same, especially when cultures amongst different demographics vary wildly, which is why the only one piece of the puzzle is enabling translation to other mediums. The other important piece to make sure communication is as efficient as possible is understanding the language from and to your translating, and how the structure of the content can be incredibly important for such a process.

Other mediums also rely on the kind of technology available. Some users may use screen readers, some may need captions for video content, or some kind of visual alert when the webpage needs more attention. When navigation or page layout is nonintuitive, for users with cognitive impairments or other forms impairments make that part of the website virtually inaccessible. The unpleasant memories that stem from being barred from something only based on what is understandable and usable to you sticks with you for a long time– humans are made to remember the negative and distressing experiences.

One example of this is someone who couldn’t find the sectional navigation on the USA today website on their ipad. Because of the visual design of the website, it wasn’t clear that the logo was a button or what the page led to. This person struggled with this webpage for a long time, and months later when this design choice was fixed, the user still recalled the navigation kerfuffle that took place months ago and was so glad at the change. Even so small a change as this can have a major impact on those that struggle with things like this.

USA Today Website before and after testing.

Instructions for navigating a website have been commonly adapted, especially with other forms of technology, and especially for users with cognitive deficits, memory to perform a task can be incredibly difficult. This is why the look, design, affordance and application of accessible and well formatted content can matter so much, and will be incredibly pivotal when so much of the available content is not inclusive enough to catch every gap.
